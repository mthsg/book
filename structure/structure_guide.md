## SECTION 1: CHAPTER OPENER (200-300 words)

### PURPOSE
Get reader interested + set expectations + signal this is practical

### STRUCTURE TEMPLATE
```
Opening (1-2 sentences): START WITH A QUESTION
├─ "Why do 75% of product launches fail?"
├─ "Your biggest competitor isn't X; it's inaction."
└─ "If you measure the wrong metric, you'll optimize for the wrong thing."

Why it matters (2-3 sentences): THE BUSINESS IMPACT
├─ "Companies that get this wrong lose $5M+ per year"
├─ "Companies that get this right achieve profitability 2x faster"
└─ "This is the #1 thing separating successful founders from average ones"

What you'll learn (3-4 bullets): THE LEARNING OUTCOMES
├─ ✓ How to identify X
├─ ✓ How to build Y framework
├─ ✓ When to use approach Z
└─ ✓ How to avoid pitfall W

How to use this (1-2 sentences): THE PRACTICAL ANGLE
└─ "By end of chapter, you'll have [TOOL] to use Monday morning"

TOTAL WORDS: 200-300
READ TIME: 1-2 minutes
```

### EXAMPLES FOR DIFFERENT CHAPTERS

**Ch. 1 (Mapping the Terrain)**
```
"Most teams size their TAM wrong. They trust analyst reports that are 18-36 months 
out of date. Or they guess. They say 'The market is $50B' without doing the math.

The cost? Founders raise money on inflated TAM assumptions, then discover the real 
market is 10x smaller. Investors lose faith. Fundraising gets harder.

Here's the thing: You can size your market correctly. It's not consultant magic. 
It's math + customer validation.

In this chapter, you'll learn to:
✓ Map profit pools (not just market size)
✓ Size TAM/SAM/SOM with precision
✓ Identify regulatory catalysts that create urgency
✓ Assess whether now is the right time to enter

By end of this chapter, you'll have a 1-page market sizing model you can 
fill out this afternoon."
```

**Ch. 3 (Who Signs the Check?)**
```
"Your best product won't sell if you're pitching to the wrong person.

You think you're selling to a VP Product. But the VP won't approve the budget unless 
the CFO agrees. And the CFO only cares about cost savings. And the CEO only cares 
about retention. And the end user only cares about ease-of-use.

Pitch the same thing to all four people, and you'll convince none of them.

In this chapter, you'll learn to:
✓ Map the entire buying committee (not just the 'buyer')
✓ Identify each persona's emotional driver
✓ Find the specific triggers that start the buying journey
✓ Build messaging that speaks to each person

By end of this chapter, you'll have a buying committee map for YOUR product, 
with specific messaging for each persona."
```

### WRITING TIPS
- Use a question (gets reader thinking)
- Be specific (not "a lot of companies fail"; say "75% fail")
- Connect to business impact ($, speed, retention)
- Make it feel achievable ("You can do this")

## SECTION 2: THE PROBLEM (400-500 words)

### PURPOSE
Show reader they're not alone in struggling + acknowledge the mistake is common + show the cost

### STRUCTURE TEMPLATE
```
Myth 1: "What most people believe" (wrong)
├─ Reality: [What's actually true]
└─ Why they believe it: [Psychology + incentive alignment]

Myth 2: [Second common mistake]
├─ Reality: [What's actually true]
└─ Why they believe it: [Founder incentive? Bias? Fear?]

Myth 3: [Third common mistake]
├─ Reality: [What's actually true]
└─ Why they believe it: [What makes this appealing to believe?]

Cost of getting it wrong:
├─ Financial cost: $X
├─ Timeline cost: Delays by Y months
└─ Strategic cost: Locks you into Z bad decision

Example (case study of failure):
└─ "[Company A] believed [myth]. They spent €5M on [consequence]. 
    What they should have done: [better approach]."

TOTAL WORDS: 400-500
READ TIME: 2-3 minutes
```

### EXAMPLE: "THE PROBLEM" for Chapter 1

```
WHAT MOST PEOPLE GET WRONG

Myth 1: "Bigger TAM = Better opportunity"
Reality: Profit pools matter more than raw market size. You can be in a $1T market 
and make zero dollars if you're in the wrong profit pool.
Why they believe it: VCs ask for TAM first. Bigger numbers make fundraising easier. 
It *feels* good to say "We're in a $50B market."

Myth 2: "If it's a huge market, we'll find a way to make money"
Reality: Market size is necessary but not sufficient. Margins, switching costs, 
and regulatory barriers matter more.
Why they believe it: Founder bias: "If the market is big enough, the law of large 
numbers will save us."

Myth 3: "Analyst reports tell us the true market size"
Reality: Analyst TAM numbers are wrong ~60% of the time. They're 18-36 months 
behind market reality.
Why they believe it: Analyst reports are credible. They have data. It's easier 
to cite a report than do the math yourself.

COST OF GETTING IT WRONG

Financial cost: Raised $20M on $50B TAM thesis. Market is actually $5B. Company 
can never reach growth targets required by board.

Timeline cost: Team spent 6 months sizing the market, realized assumptions were 
wrong in Year 2, had to pivot.

Strategic cost: Locked into GTM strategy for enterprise (high CAC) when should 
have gone PLG (low CAC). Can't pivot costs quickly enough.

FAILURE EXAMPLE: Zendesk initially sized TAM at $50B (support software market). 
But they started SMB (lower margin). When they tried to go upmarket, discovered 
enterprise needed different product. Lost 18 months repositioning.

Lesson: Zendesk didn't size profit pools within the market. They just said "total 
support software market." Cost: 2-year delay in enterprise strategy.
```

### PSYCHOLOGY ANGLE
- **Founder incentive**: Bigger numbers = easier fundraising
- **Cognitive bias**: Confirmation bias (find data supporting what you believe)
- **Team incentive**: "This is a huge market" feels better than "This is hard"

## SECTION 3: THE FRAMEWORK (800-1,200 words)

### PURPOSE
Teach the actual method step-by-step + show how pieces fit together

### STRUCTURE TEMPLATE
```
CONCEPTUAL MODEL (with visual diagram)
└─ "Here's how these 4 variables relate..."

STEP-BY-STEP BREAKDOWN
├─ Step 1: [What to do]
│  ├─ Question: [What to ask yourself]
│  ├─ Method: [How to do it]
│  ├─ Output: [What you get]
│  └─ Pitfall: [What could go wrong]
│
├─ Step 2: [What to do]
│  └─ [Same structure]
│
└─ Step 3: [What to do]
   └─ [Same structure]

KEY PRINCIPLES (decision rules)
├─ Principle 1: [Core truth about this domain]
├─ Principle 2: [Another core truth]
└─ Principle 3: [Third core truth]

COMMON PITFALLS
├─ Pitfall 1: [Easy mistake]
│  └─ Why: [Why this is tempting]
│  └─ Fix: [How to avoid it]
│
├─ Pitfall 2: [Another mistake]
│  └─ [Same structure]
│
└─ Pitfall 3: [Third mistake]
   └─ [Same structure]

TOTAL WORDS: 800-1,200
READ TIME: 4-6 minutes
```

### EXAMPLE: Framework for Chapter 1 (Mapping the Terrain)

```
CONCEPTUAL MODEL

[DIAGRAM would show:]
Market Size → TAM (Theoretically addressable)
              → SAM (Addressable with your GTM)
              → SOM (Realistic capture in 5 years)

Profit Pool → Where is money actually made?
           → Where do customers have budgets?

Market Maturity → Too early (no adoption) vs Too late (commodity)

Regulatory Catalysts → What changed? What's changing? What will change?

[All 4 must align for a good market]

STEP-BY-STEP BREAKDOWN

Step 1: MAP THE PROFIT POOL
Question: "Where do customers actually spend money in this market?"
Method:
  1. Draw value chain (supplier → manufacturer → distributor → customer)
  2. Mark where each player makes money
  3. Mark where there's friction/margin loss
  4. Identify where your product fits

Output: Profit pool map showing (1) who makes money, (2) how much, (3) where 
        your product could capture value

Pitfall: Assuming everyone in market makes similar margins. (Reality: 
         Some players are low-margin; some are high-margin. Your job is 
         to pick the right pool.)

Step 2: SIZE TAM (Top-down vs Bottom-up)
Question: "What's the total size if we won 100% of this market?"
Method (Bottom-up preferred):
  1. Identify # of potential customers (employees at companies, # of SMEs, etc.)
  2. Estimate willingness to pay (talk to 20 customers: "Would you pay $X?")
  3. Multiply: # customers × $ per customer = TAM

Method (Top-down, sanity check):
  1. Find comparable market (similar product, similar customer)
  2. Note their TAM
  3. Adjust for differences (geography, customer size, etc.)

Output: TAM with clear assumptions (e.g., "21M SMEs × €2,500/year = €52B TAM")

Pitfall: Using analyst TAM without validation. (Reality: Analysts are often 
         3 years behind. Validate with customers first.)

Step 3: SIZE SAM (Addressable by YOUR GTM model)
Question: "What portion of TAM can we reach with our go-to-market?"
Method:
  1. Start with TAM (€52B)
  2. Apply filter: "What % of market is addressable by fintech?" (say 30%)
  3. SAM = €52B × 30% = €15.6B
  4. Reality-check: "Can we reach this with our team/capital?" (Yes → Good)

Output: SAM with clear assumption (e.g., "30% of SMEs will be open to fintech")

Pitfall: SAM = TAM (assuming you'll reach everyone). (Reality: You're 1 of 5-10 
         competitors. You'll get your slice, not the whole market.)

Step 4: SIZE SOM (Realistic capture)
Question: "What's realistic for us to capture in 5 years?"
Method:
  1. Research comparable companies in your market (Stripe, Square, Adyen for payments)
  2. Note their market share (e.g., Stripe = 3% of payments market, acquired 
     over 10 years)
  3. Apply to your market: SAM × realistic market share = SOM
     (Example: €15.6B SAM × 3% = €468M SOM)

Output: SOM with realistic assumption (e.g., "We'll own 3% of fintech-addressable 
        SME market in 5 years")

Pitfall: Being too ambitious ("We'll own 20%"). (Reality: Even market leaders 
         rarely exceed 10-15% share.)

KEY PRINCIPLES

Principle 1: Profit pools > market size
└─ Big market with low margins = bad
   Small market with high margins = good
   Focus on profit pools, not headline numbers

Principle 2: TAM/SAM/SOM must be tied to specific GTM assumptions
└─ "SAM is 30% of TAM" means "We believe 30% of SMEs will switch to fintech"
   If that assumption is wrong, SAM is wrong
   Validate assumptions with customer conversations

Principle 3: Market maturity matters as much as market size
└─ $100B market that's 10 years away = bad timing
   $5B market that's 3 years from adoption = good timing
   Timing >> size

COMMON PITFALLS

Pitfall 1: Using analyst TAM without questioning
Why: Analyst reports are credible, published, easy to cite
Fix: Use analyst TAM as ceiling; do bottom-up math to validate; 
     talk to 20+ customers about willingness to pay

Pitfall 2: Confusing TAM with "maximum possible market"
Why: Easy to imagine best-case scenario
Fix: SAM = TAM you can realistically reach with your GTM
     SOM = SAM × realistic market share (1-5%, not 20%)

Pitfall 3: Ignoring regulatory headwinds
Why: Profit pools look huge until compliance costs kill margins
Fix: Add regulatory costs into margin assumption
     (Example: FinTech CAC might be higher due to compliance burden)

Pitfall 4: Not revisiting TAM as market matures
Why: Set TAM once in Series A, never revisit
Fix: Revisit annually; market moves fast
     (Example: Qonto's TAM shifted as PSD2 compliance requirements changed)
```

### VISUAL FOR THIS FRAMEWORK
```
Draw or reference:
- Value chain (supplier → manufacturer → distributor → customer)
- Waterfall: TAM → SAM → SOM (with filters)
- Maturity curve: Pre-fit → PMF → Growth → Scale → Consolidation
```

### EXAMPLE: Qonto Case Study for Chapter 1

```
COMPANY CONTEXT

Company: Qonto (fintech for SME banking)
Founded: 2016 (France)
Business Model: Monthly subscription (€9-49/month) + transaction fees
Problem: European SMEs were massively underserved by banks
Stage When Thinking This Way: Series A (2017-2018), before Series B
Why they're relevant: They're textbook example of frameworks in this chapter

HOW THEY APPLIED THE FRAMEWORK

Step 1: PROFIT POOL DISCOVERY

Qonto asked: "Where do SMEs actually spend money on banking?"

They mapped the value chain:
├─ High-margin segment: Large enterprise (€100K+/year spend with bank)
│  └─ Banks compete fiercely; margins compressed; switching costs high
│
├─ Mid-margin segment: Mid-market companies (€10K-100K/year spend)
│  └─ Banks make decent money; but not a priority for innovation
│
└─ Low-margin segment: SMEs/freelancers (<€10K/year spend)
   └─ Banks ignore them (not profitable); use spreadsheets or generic online banks
   └─ **THIS IS THE PROFIT POOL FOR QONTO**

Key insight: Traditional banks designed for high-margin, big customers. 
They couldn't profitably serve SMEs. But SMEs NEEDED better banking; 
they were stuck with pain (manual reconciliation = 5-8 hours/month).

Qonto's strategy: "Build for the profit pool everyone ignored: volume + retention."

Step 2: REGULATORY CATALYST

Qonto noticed: PSD2 (Payment Services Directive 2) came into EU law in 2015-2018.

What changed:
├─ Open banking mandate: Banks must open APIs
├─ Fintech enablement: Fintechs could now access bank data
└─ Traditional bank constraint: Legacy banks couldn't move fast on compliance

The insight: "We have a 3-5 year window before traditional banks catch up."

Qonto's strategy: Move fast, build deep, lock in SMEs before incumbents innovate.

Step 3: TAM/SAM/SOM SIZING

Qonto did bottom-up math:

TAM Calculation:
├─ Total SMEs in 8 target European countries: 21 million
├─ Average spend per SME with bank: €2,500-3,000/year
├─ Total market: 21M × €2,500 = €52.5 billion TAM
└─ Sanity check: Looked at European banking market reports (€60B+) ✓

SAM Calculation:
├─ Total TAM: €52.5B
├─ Assumption: 30% of SMEs will eventually switch to fintech (from banks + spreadsheets)
├─ SAM: €52.5B × 30% = €15.75 billion SAM
└─ Reality-check: "Can fintech reach 30%?" Validated with 50 SME interviews. ✓

SOM Calculation:
├─ SAM: €15.75B
├─ Realistic market share: 3% (Stripe reference: 3% of payments market)
├─ SOM: €15.75B × 3% = €472.5 million SOM
└─ Translation: If Qonto captures 3% of fintech-addressable SME market, €472M ARR

Qonto's actual path:
├─ Year 3 (2019): €3M ARR (achieved)
├─ Year 5 (2021): €10M ARR (achieved)
├─ Year 7 (2023): €60M ARR (achieved)
├─ Year 8 (2024): €100M ARR (estimated)
└─ On track to hit €400-500M ARR by 2026-2027 (SOM assumption holds)

Step 4: MARKET MATURITY

Qonto assessed:
├─ 2015-2018: Pre-product fit (SMEs wanted better banking, but no solution existed)
├─ 2018-2022: Growth (Qonto validating PMF, market accepting fintech)
├─ 2022+: Competition entering (N26, Revolut, Wise all competing)
└─ 2025+: Market becoming more competitive

Qonto's decision: "We're in Year 3-4 of a 5-year window. Must go deep before 
                    competition enters. Expand to 8 countries + add invoicing."

RESULTS

What Qonto achieved:
├─ 600K+ customers by 2024 (from 0)
├─ €60M+ ARR by 2023 (from €0)
├─ €505M Series C raised (2025), at ~$2.5B valuation
├─ Expanded to 8 European countries
├─ Added invoicing (raising ARPU from €15 to €25)
├─ Added spend management (raising ARPU to €30+)
├─ Pursued banking license (expanding TAM upmarket)

Validation that framework worked:
├─ TAM sizing (€52B) proved accurate (other fintechs in same market)
├─ SAM assumption (30% fintech addressable) tracking well (Revolut, N26, Wise all in market)
├─ SOM assumption (3% capture) still realistic (Qonto at 600K, addressable SME market ~20M+)
├─ Market maturity perfectly timed (entered pre-competition, expanded as market grew)

LESSON FOR YOUR BUSINESS

"Qonto's key insight: They didn't just size the market; they sized the profit pool. 
They realized traditional banks had ignored SMEs not because the segment was too 
small, but because banks' cost structure couldn't serve it profitably.

Qonto built a DIFFERENT cost structure (freemium → high-touch → product-led), 
enabling them to serve that profit pool profitably.

Apply this to your market:
1. Where are competitors NOT serving customers?
2. Is it because market is small, or because their model can't serve it?
3. Can you build a cost structure that DOES serve them profitably?
4. If yes, you've found your profit pool."
```

### EXAMPLE: Back Market for Chapter 1

```
COMPANY CONTEXT: Back Market (Different from Qonto because B2C marketplace)

Back Market: Refurbished electronics marketplace
Founded: 2014
Why different: Consumer-facing marketplace, not B2B SaaS; waste category, not finance

HOW FRAMEWORK DIFFERS

Profit Pool (Step 1):
├─ Qonto: Money from SME banking relationships
├─ Back Market: Money from marketplace friction (% of GMV as commission)
└─ Insight: "Profit pool is in transaction volume, not high-value deals"

Regulatory Catalyst (Step 2):
├─ Qonto: PSD2 (financial regulation)
├─ Back Market: WEEE directive (environmental regulation, 2015)
└─ Impact: "WEEE made refurbishment COMPLIANT + created ESG buyer segment"

TAM Sizing (Step 3):
├─ Qonto: "21M SMEs × €2,500/year banking spend"
├─ Back Market: Different calculation entirely:
│  ├─ Global e-waste: 53.6M metric tons/year (2022)
│  ├─ Only 17% recycled = 80%+ waste (opportunity)
│  ├─ 36% of tech sales globally could be refurbished (if quality controlled)
│  └─ TAM: $1.2T+ (global e-commerce) but real TAM = hidden in waste
│
└─ Implication: "TAM is 100x bigger than traditional e-commerce, but harder to see"

Market Maturity (Step 4):
├─ Qonto: 2018-2022 was growth phase (clear need, solving it)
├─ Back Market: 2014-2018 was "convince market that refurbished = good"
│  ├─ Consumer skepticism high
│  ├─ Sustainability trend accelerating (2018-2022)
│  └─ Supply-chain disruptions made refurbished aspirational (2021-2022)
│
└─ Timing: "Back Market hit market EARLIER than Qonto, had to educate market"

KEY INSIGHT SPECIFIC TO BACK MARKET

"Back Market's genius: They identified a profit pool in a 'waste' category 
by connecting two trends:
1. Environmental regulation (WEEE) making it legal/compliant
2. Supply-chain disruptions making it desirable

They didn't try to go mass-market consumer; they went B2B first 
(enterprises with ESG targets), which had higher margins.

For your business: Look for hidden TAM in 'waste' or 'low-value' 
categories where regulatory changes + market trends align."

LESSON FOR YOUR SITUATION

"If you're in a category people think is 'commodity' or 'waste':
1. Is there a regulatory trend? (WEEE for e-waste, GDPR for data, etc.)
2. Is there a values/ESG trend? (Sustainability, privacy, ethical sourcing)
3. Can you connect them? (Compliant + desirable = premium positioning)

Back Market did this masterfully. They reframed 'second-hand electronics' 
as 'sustainable tech' and opened an entirely new buyer segment 
(enterprises, not just price-conscious consumers)."
```

## FINAL CHAPTER CHECKLIST

Before you finish writing:

✓ **Opener**: Did you start with a compelling question?
✓ **Problem**: Did you show what goes wrong (so readers feel seen)?
✓ **Framework**: Do you have 3-4 clear steps?
✓ **Example 1**: Do you show primary company applying framework?
✓ **Example 2**: Do you show different context (not just repeating)?
✓ **Tools**: Do you have 2-3 worksheets readers can use immediately?
✓ **Assessment**: Do you have diagnostic so readers assess themselves?
✓ **Bridge**: Do you explain how this chapter leads to next?
✓ **Visuals**: Do you have 2-3 visuals (one per 500-800 words)?
✓ **Tone**: Is it conversational (not academic)?

If all 10: Your chapter is ready to ship.
